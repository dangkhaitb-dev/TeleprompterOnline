Talking-Head Video Script Template: Hook, Value, and Close

A talking-head video script needs a clear promise, a useful explanation, and a close that tells the viewer what to do next. Replace the bracketed prompts, keep each section focused, and rehearse the result aloud before recording.

Reviewed by the SolPrompter team · Updated August 23, 2026

Open SolPrompter

A talking-head video script needs a clear promise, a useful explanation, and a close that tells the viewer what to do with the information. Copy the template below, replace the bracketed prompts with your subject, and rehearse it aloud before you record. Write for one viewer, not for a page.

This is a structure for a direct-to-camera video. It does not replace the teleprompter-formatting guidance in How to Write a Teleprompter Script, or the delivery practice in Speaking on Camera.

Copy this talking-head video script template

Use this as a fill-in framework. The sections have different jobs, so avoid using the hook to explain everything or the close to introduce a new idea.

  • 1. Hook: [0:00–0:15] Name a specific problem, tension, or useful outcome: “If [audience] keeps running into [problem], this is the one change that can help.”
  • 2. Promise and context: [0:15–0:30] Say what the viewer will learn and why it matters now. Keep only the context needed to understand the advice.
  • 3. Main value: [middle] Teach two to four points in a logical order. For each point: name it, explain it, then give an example, consequence, or demonstration.
  • 4. Visual beat: [where useful] Mark a screen, object, graphic, or pause that helps the viewer process the point. Do not add a visual only to fill time.
  • 5. Recap and close: [last 10–20 seconds] Restate the practical takeaway and give one next action that follows naturally from the video.

Choose one viewer and one outcome

Before drafting, complete two sentences: “This video is for [specific person]” and “After watching, they can [observable result].” The result should describe a decision or action, not a vague feeling of awareness.

Weak outcome: “Viewers understand teleprompters.” Strong outcome: “First-time presenters can choose a reading method for a two-minute camera update.”

One outcome gives you a useful editing rule: if a section does not help the viewer reach it, move that material to another video or resource. It also prevents this template from overlapping with a broad presentation script or a teleprompter-formatting guide.

Write a hook that makes a specific promise

The hook should quickly connect the video to a real viewer problem or desired result. It does not need a dramatic claim. A precise promise is stronger than a vague “today we're talking about” introduction because it helps the right viewer decide whether to keep watching.

  • Problem hook: “If your camera updates sound rehearsed, the problem may be the script layout—not your voice.”
  • Outcome hook: “In the next three minutes, you will turn one messy update into a script you can read naturally.”
  • Mistake hook: “Most short product videos lose clarity when they explain the feature before naming the user problem.”

YouTube's retention report specifically calls out the first 30 seconds and compares how many viewers are still watching with typical videos of similar length. That makes the opening worth testing against the promise in the title and thumbnail, rather than treating it as a formality. Read YouTube's audience-retention guidance.

Give each main point one job

A useful talking-head section follows a small teaching loop: state the point, explain why it matters, and make it concrete. This keeps a video from becoming a list of unsupported tips.

Point: “Time the full rehearsal.” Why: “An audio-only read misses the pauses and screen actions that use up runtime.” Example: “In a five-minute walkthrough, reserve the demo time before you calculate a word budget.”

Use two to four main points when that is enough to deliver the promise. Add another point only when it changes what the viewer should do. If your message needs more sections, it may be a series, a presentation, or a longer tutorial rather than a single talking-head video.

Add visual beats without writing a second script

A talking-head video can remain direct-to-camera while using a few purposeful visual beats. Put a short bracketed production note where a screen, close-up, caption, or pause will make the spoken point easier to understand. Keep it separate from the words you will say.

  • [Show] Use for a screen recording, object, slide, or before-and-after example.
  • [On-screen text] Use for a short phrase or number that viewers should retain; do not transcribe the full paragraph.
  • [Pause] Use after a key idea or before a visual change when the viewer needs a moment to process it.
  • [Cut] Use to remove a reset, switch angle, or move to a new section without making the spoken draft awkward.

If the visual plan becomes central to the message, use a two-column audio/visual script instead. The Defense Information School describes the distinction between simple scripts and formats that coordinate visuals with audio. See the format overview.

Filled example: a short script about camera updates

This original example demonstrates the structure. Its fictional workplace scenario is not a performance claim or a prescribed set of words.

  • Hook “If your weekly camera update turns into a list of tasks, try one change: lead with the decision your viewer needs to make.”
  • Promise “In the next two minutes, I will show a three-line structure that makes the priority, owner, and deadline easier to hear.”
  • Point one “Start with the decision. Say what needs approval, a choice, or attention before you give background.” [On-screen text: Decision first]
  • Point two “Then name the owner. A task without a named decision-maker sounds like an update without a next step.”
  • Point three “Finally, give the deadline and the consequence of waiting. That helps the viewer decide what to do now.” [Show: a three-line example]
  • Close “For your next update, write the decision, owner, and deadline before you add any background. The rest of the message will be easier to organize.”

Write for the ear, then format for the display

A full script should sound like something you would say to one person. Use short, direct sentences and read the draft aloud. Broadcast-writing guidance makes the same distinction: spoken material is heard in sequence, so it benefits from conversational language and clear sentences. Read the University of Florida guidance.

Once the wording works, format it for the teleprompter with line breaks, pronunciation notes, and intentional pauses. That delivery formatting belongs in the teleprompter script guide; it is not a reason to add those mechanics to every first draft.

Time the script after the first full draft

Do not pick a generic pace and force the script to match it. Record a representative rehearsal in your real setup, including visual beats and natural pauses. Use your measured delivery rate to set a word budget, then revise until the complete run fits.

The Teleprompter Script Word Count and Timing Guide provides the calculation and a planning worksheet. For a video with slides, use the separate Presentation Script Template so narration and slide transitions have their own structure.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a script for a talking-head video?

Use a full script when wording, timing, or accuracy matters; use notes when you know the subject and need flexibility. A hybrid is often effective: script the hook, critical claims, and close, then use bullets for familiar examples.

How long should a talking-head video script be?

It should fit the available speaking time at your measured rehearsal rate, after you reserve time for visual beats and pauses. Run a full rehearsal to confirm the real runtime rather than relying on a universal words-per-minute figure.

What should I say at the end of a talking-head video?

Restate the useful takeaway and give one next action that follows from it. The close should finish the promise made in the hook, not introduce a new subject or a generic call to action.
