Video Publishing Checklist: Export, Captions, and Upload

Publish a video by confirming the final cut and export match the destination, checking captions and metadata, uploading early enough for processing, then reviewing the version viewers can actually watch. Keep the source files and a record of the published version so corrections do not require rebuilding the project from scratch.

Confirm the destination before export

A video that looks good locally can still be the wrong file for its destination. Check the platform or client requirements for orientation, aspect ratio, duration, file type, resolution, captions, privacy, and delivery date. Use the current requirements of the actual destination rather than copying settings from an unrelated project.

For example, YouTube’s upload guidance distinguishes uploading from publishing and notes that higher-resolution processing can take longer. Its recommended upload-encoding guidance should be used only when YouTube is the destination. YouTube: Upload videos · YouTube: Recommended upload encoding settings.

Review the final cut and captions

Watch the exported file before upload, including its first seconds and closing frame. Confirm that the selected version is complete, the sound is understandable, the picture has the intended framing, and the captions belong to this final edit. If you use automatic captions, review them for names, numbers, technical terms, and any sentence where an error changes the meaning.

For web content seeking WCAG 2.2 Level A conformance, prerecorded media with meaningful audio needs synchronized captions, subject to the criterion’s media-alternative exception. Captions, a transcript or media alternative, and audio description solve different access needs; whether you need more than captions depends on the information carried only in the audio or visuals and on the conformance target. W3C: Captions (Prerecorded) · W3C: Audio Description or Media Alternative.

Write metadata that makes the promise clear

Give the destination the information a viewer needs to decide whether the video is for them: an accurate title, a concise description, an appropriate thumbnail, and any required audience or privacy setting. Match the title and thumbnail promise to the opening of the video. Do not use metadata to imply an outcome the video does not actually deliver.

On YouTube, titles have a 100-character limit and descriptions have a 5,000-character limit. Those are platform-specific limits, not general writing targets. YouTube upload details guidance.

Upload early and review the published version

Upload before the deadline whenever the destination permits it. Processing, caption generation, checks, and a final review can take time. Once the video is ready, play the version served by the host on a typical viewer device or browser. A local export cannot show you every issue introduced by hosting, processing, privacy settings, or an incomplete upload.

  • Check that the intended visibility and audience settings are correct.
  • Open the video from the public or viewer-facing link, not only the upload dashboard.
  • Turn captions on and confirm they are synchronized with the final cut.
  • Check the title, description, thumbnail, chapters, links, and any required attribution.
  • If the destination creates multiple resolutions, wait until the required quality is available before sharing the link.

Keep source files and a release record

Retain the original footage, editable project, final export, caption file or source, thumbnail, and the published link. Note the publication date and version. This makes a correction, re-upload, format change, or derivative clip a focused update instead of a reconstruction from memory.

Create a destination release brief

Before export, write down the destination’s actual requirements: aspect ratio and orientation, maximum duration, required file or codec, caption format, privacy or audience setting, title and description needs, thumbnail, publishing time, and any approver. This separates universal release work from settings that belong only to a particular host.

Name files so the release can be traced

Use a filename that identifies the project, orientation or version, and date when that helps your team distinguish exports. Keep the editable project, the approved master, captions, thumbnail, and final hosted link together. A release record should tell a future editor which exact file was published and where.

Frequently asked questions

How early should I upload a video?

Early enough to let the destination process the file, run any checks, and give you time to review the viewer-facing version. The exact allowance depends on the host, file size, resolution, account status, and deadline, so use the destination’s current guidance rather than a fixed universal number.

Can I change a video after upload?

Some hosts let you change details such as title, description, thumbnail, captions, or visibility, while a change to the video itself may require a replacement or new upload. Check the host’s current rules and retain the source files so a correction is possible.

How do I check the published version?

Open the viewer-facing link in a typical browser or device, play the beginning, a middle section, and the end, enable captions, and check the title, thumbnail, descriptions, links, and privacy behavior. If the video has a scheduled release, repeat the check after it becomes available to the intended audience.

Video publishing checklist

  • The destination’s current technical, timing, privacy, and accessibility requirements are known.
  • The final exported file has been watched and heard before upload.
  • Captions are synchronized and reviewed for meaningful errors.
  • The title, description, thumbnail, and audience settings accurately represent the video.
  • The upload leaves time for processing, checks, and a viewer-facing review.
  • The published version has been played from the intended viewer-facing link.
  • Originals, project files, captions, final export, and release details are retained.

Next step

For the next video, return to planning with the same viewer outcome and use what you learned from the published version to improve the next shoot.
